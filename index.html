Why Video Upscaling Is Not Image Upscaling, Repeated 300 Times

A video upscaler that works frame by frame is not a video upscaler. It is an image upscaler called in a loop, and the failure it produces is invisible on any single frame by construction.

14 November 2026 · 40-frame sub-pixel pan, ffmpeg + numpy

The pitch is always the same: "upscale every frame, get a 4K video." Technically true. Practically, what you get is a sequence of 4K frames that do not agree with each other about what the scene contains, and your visual system is extremely good at noticing disagreement even when it cannot name it.

I built a test to isolate this from every other variable: a static, high-detail scene, panned by 0.2 pixels per frame. Nothing in the scene moves. Nothing appears or disappears. The only thing changing is the sub-pixel phase of the source.

The mechanism, before the numbers

When you shrink an image and then enlarge it, each output pixel is a weighted average of source pixels. The weights depend on where the output pixel falls relative to the source grid. Pan by a fraction of a pixel and those weights change. On a hard edge, a small weight change flips a pixel from mostly-dark to mostly-light.

In a still image that is invisible — you have one phase, you see one result. In a video you have a different phase every frame, so the same patch of wall gets a slightly different edge on every frame. Played at 24 fps, edges shimmer. This is the artifact people describe as "boiling" or "crawling," and it is a property of the resampling kernel, not of the content.

What I measured, and what surprised me

Metric: high-frequency energy per frame, computed as the ratio of 2-D FFT magnitude outside a radius of 0.18 × width to the magnitude inside it. For a smooth pan, this should be nearly constant. Its coefficient of variation (CV) across 40 frames is the instability.

Source at 160px2.45350.0347 1.41%2.385–2.523

The surprise is in the last two columns of the middle row. The per-frame upscale is more temporally stable than the ground truth — CV 2.87% versus 5.99%. That looks like it contradicts everything I just said, so it is worth being precise about why it does not.

Stability is not a virtue when it comes from destroying the signal. Lanczos keeps 21.5% of the ground truth's high-frequency energy at 2× (measured separately). It is stable because there is very little left to fluctuate. A method that preserves detail has more detail available to flicker, and it will.

So the measurement refutes the naive version of the claim. Per-frame interpolation does not flicker more than reality does — it flickers less, by flattening the scene until there is nothing left to flicker. The flicker problem is real, but it belongs to the methods that actually try to reconstruct detail, not to the ones that blur.

Where the instability actually comes from

Three sources, in descending order of how much damage they do:

  1. Reconstruction models. A generative upscaler samples from a distribution. Run it twice on nearly-identical inputs and it can pick different plausible details — a different hair strand, a different texture direction. At 24 fps that is a scene that reinvents itself every frame. This is the dominant cause of the artifact people complain about, and it is invisible in a single-frame comparison.
  2. Sharpening steps. Unsharp mask is nonlinear near edges. Small changes in input produce discontinuous changes in output right at the edge, which is exactly where the eye is looking. (Measured below: on an isolated edge this turned out not to hold — sharpening reduced positional spread. The nonlinearity is real; the direction I assumed for it was not.)
  3. Per-frame noise reduction. Any denoiser with a threshold behaves differently on either side of that threshold. Frames straddling it get visibly different treatment.

Plain interpolation — the thing in the table above — is the one case that is stable, because it is a fixed linear filter. It is also the case that adds nothing. That trade is the whole subject.

What to do about it

Not "use a different kernel." The kernel is not the problem. Practical options, in order of how much they help:

  • Give the model temporal context. A model that sees frames n−1 and n+1 when producing frame n can hold detail consistent across the shot. This is the only real fix and it is why video-specific models beat image models run in a loop.
  • Reduce the scale factor. At 2×, Lanczos keeps 21.5% of high-frequency energy; at 4× it keeps 1.3%. Less aggressive scaling means less to invent means less to disagree about.
  • Test on motion, not on a still. Extract three consecutive frames around a hard edge and compare them. If the edge sits in a different place in each, the method will shimmer, no matter how good frame 1 looks.
  • Accept the trade explicitly. A softer, temporally stable result is often better viewing than a sharp still that boils in motion. That is a real engineering choice, not a compromise.

The metric I was using can be gamed

Everything above rests on one number: the coefficient of variation of global high-frequency energy across frames. Before trusting the advice built on it, I tried to break it, and it broke easily. The failure is worth reporting because it changes what the table above actually means.

Two problems. First, HF energy is a global scalar: one number per frame. A method that adds detail uniformly and a method that swaps detail locally produce identical scalars, but only the second one crawls, because the eye tracks local correspondence, not total energy. Second, and worse, the metric has a trivial optimum — blur the frame toward a constant and the variance goes to zero. A quietly wrong upscaler scores as the most stable one.

So I rebuilt the measurement on the same 40-frame pan, same scene, same step of 0.20 px, with three metrics that fail in different directions:

  • HF energy — high-frequency magnitude outside r = 0.18 × width, as a percentage of the ground-truth frame at the same phase.
  • Flicker — mean per-pixel |HPt − HPt−1| inside a σ = 1.0 gaussian high-pass, divided by mean |HP|. Local instead of global.
  • HF correlation — Pearson correlation between the upscaled frame's high-pass band and the real frame's high-pass band at the same phase. This one cannot be won by adding energy or by removing it. It can only be won by being right.

Look at the first row. Nearest-neighbour has the highest high-frequency energy of any method — 56.3% of ground truth, more than double Lanczos — because blocky aliasing invents high-frequency content that was never in the scene. It also has the lowest flicker of any method, 15.54%. It wins both of the metrics I had been quoting. It also has the worst agreement with reality in the table, 0.2883, and a correlation that swings by 0.4245 across the pan — the largest spread of any method here.

The two numbers that made it look good are the two it cheats on. If a method wins on energy and on stability at the same time as losing on correlation, it is not stable — it is wrong in a way that holds still.

Lanczos has the lowest correlation spread by a factor of two (0.1001 versus 0.2138 for bicubic) and the highest mean correlation. That is the property that actually matters in motion: not "does this frame have detail", but "does it have the same detail as the frame before". Bicubic's mean correlation is nearly identical — 0.4000 against 0.4153 — but its spread is twice as large, and spread is what the eye reads as crawling.

This reframes the earlier table rather than contradicting it. The CV numbers were real; I was reading a stability metric where I should have been reading a fidelity metric. Global stability minus fidelity is the definition of the failure mode this article is about.

The flicker is not in the edge position

My first hypothesis was geometric: if the sub-pixel phase changes, the upscaled edge should land in a slightly different place each frame, and that displacement is the crawl. It is a clean hypothesis and it is wrong, and the way it is wrong is useful.

Test: one isolated vertical step edge, blurred by σ = 1.0 so the position estimator has something to integrate, rendered at 40 phases spanning exactly one pixel, shrunk 2×, upscaled. Position is the centroid of |d/dx| over a ±10 px window; error is measured against the ground truth frame at the same phase.

Source, 160px, no upscale0.5670.643 −0.5650.142
Nearest 2×0.0830.143 −0.0650.142
Lanczos 2×0.0920.134 0.0070.257
Bicubic 2×0.0680.102 0.0040.183
Bicubic + USM r1.2 140%0.056 0.0910.044 0.094

All four upscalers place the edge within 0.092 px RMS of where it belongs. That is a tenth of a pixel — sub-pixel enough that no display can show it as a displaced edge. The un-upscaled source is the outlier at 0.567 px with a consistent −0.565 px bias, which is just the half-sample offset of a 2× downscale, and it is the one sequence that does not crawl. Edge displacement is therefore not the mechanism. Whatever the eye is seeing is happening inside the edge, in how its profile changes shape, not in where its centre sits.

One incidental finding worth keeping: unsharp masking improves edge position accuracy, 0.092 → 0.056 px RMS and 0.257 → 0.094 px of phase spread, because it re-steepens the profile the interpolation rounded off. The sharpening step is not creating the jitter here. It is compensating for it, which is the opposite of what the ordering in my earlier list of culprits suggested.

What "reduce the scale factor" is actually worth

The article says a smaller scale factor helps because there is less to invent. That is qualitative, so I measured it: identical scene, identical kernels, 2× and 4×. To compare fairly I report detail per unit of crawl — flicker divided by HF energy, where lower is better — because a method can always lower flicker by lowering detail.

ScaleHF energyFlicker flicker / energy
2× Lanczos26.8%16.24% 0.61
4× Lanczos8.0%16.55% 2.08
2× Bicubic26.7%16.14% 0.61
4× Bicubic8.2%15.90% 1.95

Going from 2× to 4× costs 3.4× in detail per unit of flicker for both kernels (0.61 → 2.08). The mechanism is visible in the middle column: flicker barely moves, 16.24% → 16.55%, while detail collapses, 26.8% → 8.0%. At 4× the interpolation is not introducing instability; it is introducing so little detail that instability has nothing to act on, and the ratio is what exposes that. The advice holds, and now it has a magnitude.

And the caveat the ratio itself demands: nearest-neighbour scores 0.28 at 2× and 0.25 at 4×, better than ground truth's 0.26, purely by having the lowest flicker and inflated energy. Any "detail per crawl" figure has to be gated behind the correlation column, or it will recommend the ugliest option in the table. That is the same trap as the original metric, one level up, and I only found it because I ran the correlation.

The flicker I was measuring was the camera moving

Everything above compares kernels. This section asks a different question: is the frame-to-frame number I have been calling "instability" about the resampling at all? The test holds the scene fixed, holds the kernel fixed at area-integrated Lanczos3, and changes exactly one thing — how far the content moves between frames. If the metric is measuring the kernel, it should not move. It moves by 18×.

Scene: one continuous drawing function, 512 world units wide, sampled on a 256² source grid (pitch 2.0 world units) and resampled 2× to 512². It contains three hard edges, a near-Nyquist stripe pattern, and a slow luminance ramp. The pan is horizontal, sub-pixel, and continuous. Ground truth is the same drawing function sampled directly on the 512² grid at the same world offset. Metric is mean absolute error per frame against that ground truth, and the spread is the coefficient of variation of that per-frame MAE across 40 frames.

Same kernel in every row. CV(MAE) goes from 1.87% to 33.81% — a factor of 18.1 — purely by panning faster. And the second column explains it: MAE falls as the pan speeds up, 14.84 to 9.81. The error is not getting noisier. It is getting smaller, and the ratio it is being divided by is shrinking, so the coefficient of variation inflates. That is an artefact of normalising a changing quantity by itself, not a property of Lanczos.

The reason MAE falls is that the content is leaving the frame. Over 40 frames the pan translates the scene by 40 × v source pixels, so the composition at frame 40 is not the composition at frame 1 — the hard edges have walked out of the visible area and been replaced by flat regions that any resampler reproduces almost exactly.

So the quantity my earlier table called flicker was, at least in part, the scene emptying out. I want to be careful about what this does and does not overturn. The original CV was computed on FFT high-frequency energy, not on MAE against ground truth, and on a scene built to stay in frame — so the numbers above are not retroactively invalidating it. What they do invalidate is the reading I was putting on it. A CV of an image statistic across a moving sequence mixes two things: how the resampler handles a given sub-pixel phase, and how much structure happens to be in shot. Those are not separable by staring at the single number.

Four kernels, same pan, and the number barely notices

The sharpest test is the one I should have run first. Hold the pan at 1.13 source px/frame and swap only the reconstruction kernel. Nearest, bilinear, bicubic and Lanczos3 differ by about 4.5 units of MAE — a real difference, the one that matters — but their CV(MAE) is the same number to three decimal places.

Spread across the four kernels: 1.0020×, i.e. 0.12 percentage points. Pan speed over the same metric: 18.1×. The variable that dominates the "instability" number by two orders of magnitude is not the resampling filter. It is translation. Any tuning loop that uses frame-to-frame variation as its objective function is, first and foremost, optimising how fast the camera moves.

Why the error cannot flicker when the pan is an integer

A separable resampling matrix is translation-invariant in its interior: shifting the input by one source pixel shifts the output by exactly two output pixels, bit for bit. Verified directly — the maximum difference between the shifted output and the true roll is 0.0, not merely small. The only exception is the frame border, where taps fall outside the array.

The consequence is exact rather than statistical. If the content advances by a whole number of source pixels per frame, then frame n+1 is frame n translated, and the error field against ground truth is the same function translated by the same amount. The temporal difference between them is zero.

Zero, exactly, in all three cases. There is no per-frame error variation to see at these speeds — and yet the CV column in the first table reports 8.11%, 18.97% and 33.81%. The metric is reporting motion. It has to: at integer pan the frames differ only by where the content sits, and MAE against an unshifted ground truth absorbs that as a steadily changing number.

Non-integer pan is the interesting case, and it is the only one where genuine per-phase variation exists. At 1.13 px/frame the error field does not repeat under any integer shift — best-effort residuals of 8.2 to 18.5 across 2, 3 and 4 px. That is the real sampling-phase signature, and it is what you would want to measure. Distinguishing it from the composition drift requires either holding the composition fixed or aligning frames before differencing, and neither is what a raw CV does.

What this changes about the advice

It removes one candidate explanation and leaves the rest standing. Kernel choice is not the lever — 0.12 points of CV across four filters that differ by 4.5 points of actual error. Scale factor still is: at 2× the upscale's high-frequency CV tracks ground truth at 0.94×, and at 2.41 px/frame it is 2.35× tighter than ground truth, which is the same "stable because flattened" trap the earlier section describes, just measured against a proper baseline. The detail that survives is still governed by the scale factor, as the flicker-per-energy ratio already showed.

Practical version, for anyone testing their own footage: do not compare CV across clips with different motion, and do not compare it across two pan speeds of the same clip. Compare it across kernels at one fixed speed, and difference the error rather than the image. If you only have the image, align before subtracting, or the measurement is mostly reporting what walked out of shot.

Two bugs this cost me

Border normalisation. My first resampling matrix normalised the kernel weights before discarding taps that fell outside the array. A flat field of 100 came back ranging from 54.99 to 110.95 — a 45-unit error at the corner, and every frame in the sequence inherited it. The fix is one line: zero out-of-range taps before dividing by the sum. After it, a flat field returns exactly 100.000 everywhere. The interior numbers above were unchanged by the fix, which is how I know the defect was border-only — but a 45-unit artefact at the frame edge is more than enough to dominate a metric computed over the whole image, and it is the kind of thing that looks like a discovery until you test it on a constant.

Half-pixel ground-truth offset. The resampler places output pixel i at source coordinate (i + 0.5)/scale − 0.5, so output pixel centres sit half a pixel inside the ground-truth grid I was sampling. Measured against a naive grid the MAE floor on smooth content was 0.73; with the correct −0.5 offset it is 0.016, a factor of 46. Every kernel comparison earlier in this page that reports MAE is only meaningful after this correction, and before finding it I had "measured" a 0.73-unit error that was pure indexing.

Reproduce the speed axis

import numpy as np

def lanczos3(x):
    a = np.abs(x); out = np.zeros_like(x); m = a < 3.0
    out[m] = np.sinc(a[m]) * np.sinc(a[m] / 3.0)
    return out

def resample_matrix(n_in, scale, kernel, support, sub=16):
    # integrate the kernel over each source pixel cell;
    # drop out-of-range taps BEFORE normalising, or the border gets a wrong DC gain
    n_out = int(round(n_in * scale)); step = 1.0 / scale
    W = np.zeros((n_out, n_in))
    s = (np.arange(sub) + 0.5) / sub - 0.5
    for i in range(n_out):
        u = (i + 0.5) * step - 0.5
        idx = np.arange(int(np.floor(u - support)) - 1, int(np.ceil(u + support)) + 2)
        w = np.array([kernel(idx[k] - u + s).mean() for k in range(len(idx))])
        w = w * ((idx >= 0) & (idx < n_in)); w /= w.sum()
        for j, wv in zip(idx, w):
            if 0 <= j < n_in: W[i, j] += wv
    return W

NN, WORLD, OUT = 256, 512.0, 512
SRC = WORLD / NN
W = resample_matrix(NN, 2.0, lanczos3, 3.0)

def scene(x, y):
    v = 140.0 + 10 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * x / 700.0)
    for xc in (120.0, 250.0, 380.0):
        v = v + np.where(x >= xc, -70.0, 0.0)
    v = v + 22 * np.sign(np.sin(2*np.pi*(x/2.6) + 2*np.pi*(y/300.0)))
    return np.clip(v, 0, 255)

def panel(n, ox=0.0):
    p = WORLD / n
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(ox + np.arange(n)*p, np.arange(n)*p)
    return scene(X, Y)

# the -0.5 is the half-pixel ground-truth offset described above; drop it and MAE
# inflates from 0.016 to 0.73 on smooth content
for v in (0.25, 1.0, 3.0):
    mae = []
    for n in range(40):
        ox = v * n * SRC
        up = W @ panel(NN, ox) @ W.T
        gt = panel(OUT, ox - 0.5)
        mae.append(np.abs(up - gt)[60:-60, 60:-60].mean())
    mae = np.array(mae)
    print(f'{v:5.2f} px/frame  CV(MAE) {mae.std(ddof=1)/mae.mean()*100:6.2f}%  MAE {mae.mean():6.2f}')

That prints 2.30%, 10.47% and 45.67% for the three speeds at a 60-pixel interior margin — the same monotone climb as the table, reproduced from a standalone script with no shared state. The margin matters: without cropping the border, the edge-normalisation defect adds a constant to every frame and depresses the spread.

Reproducing it

from PIL import Image, ImageFilter
import numpy as np

# 40-frame sub-pixel pan, 320x320 working size, shift = i * 0.20 px per frame.
# The source is rendered at 320 and shrunk to 160 -- what a real downscale does.
LANCZOS = Image.LANCZOS


def scene_at(size, dx, period):
    n = size
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:n, 0:n].astype(float)
    x = xx + dx
    v = 140.0 + 10 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * x / 176.0)
    for xc in (40.0, 96.0, 150.0):                      # hard edges
        v = v + np.where(x >= xc, -70.0, 0.0)
    if period:                                          # near-Nyquist stripes
        v = v + 22 * np.sign(np.sin(2 * np.pi * x / period))
    return np.clip(v, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)


def hf_ratio(a):
    a = a.astype(float) - a.mean()
    F = np.abs(np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2(a)))
    h, w = a.shape
    Y, X = np.mgrid[0:h, 0:w]
    r = np.hypot(Y - h // 2, X - w // 2)
    hi = r > 0.18 * w
    return F[hi].sum() / max(F[~hi].sum(), 1e-12)


def cv(v):
    v = np.asarray(v, float)
    return v.std(ddof=1) / v.mean() * 100


for period in (2.6, 6.0, None):
    gt_hf, up_hf = [], []
    for i in range(40):
        gt = scene_at(320, i * 0.20, period)
        lo = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(gt).resize((160, 160), LANCZOS))
        up = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(lo).resize((320, 320), LANCZOS))
        gt_hf.append(hf_ratio(gt))
        up_hf.append(hf_ratio(up))
    tag = 'no stripes' if period is None else f'stripes {period}px'
    print(f'{tag:>16s}  CV(HF) GT {cv(gt_hf):6.2f}%   upscale {cv(up_hf):6.2f}%'
          f'   up/GT {cv(up_hf)/cv(gt_hf):.2f}x')


# --- and the metrics that can't be gamed the same way ---
def highpass(a):
    a = a.astype(float)
    im = Image.fromarray(a.astype('uint8'))
    return a - np.asarray(im.filter(ImageFilter.GaussianBlur(1.0)), float)


gt = scene_at(320, 0.0, 2.6)
lo = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(gt).resize((160, 160), LANCZOS))
up = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(lo).resize((320, 320), LANCZOS))
hf_corr = np.corrcoef(highpass(up).ravel(), highpass(gt).ravel())[0, 1]
print('HF correlation vs ground truth %.3f' % hf_corr)

That second block is the honest version of the reproduction, and it prints a result that contradicts the table at the top of this page. It is meant to. The next section explains why, and it is the most useful thing on the page.

Where the earlier conclusion breaks, and why

The second snippet above prints something that contradicts the table at the top of this page. With hard edges in the scene it reports CV(HF) 4.85% for ground truth against 8.46% for the per-frame upscale — the upscale is less stable, the ordinary expectation. The earlier table reports the opposite. Both are real measurements of the same metric on different scenes, and the difference between the scenes is one term.

Scene contentsCV(HF) GTCV(HF) up up / GT
Ramp only4.96%4.61%0.93×
Ramp + hard edges1.50%1.48%0.98×
Ramp + near-Nyquist stripes2.51%18.37% 7.31×
Ramp + edges + stripes4.85%8.46% 1.75×

Strip the stripes out and the upscale is marginally more stable than ground truth, which is the effect the earlier table described. Add them back and it is 1.75× to 7.31× less stable. Every bit of the discrepancy lives in the near-Nyquist content, and the mechanism is not subtle once the term is isolated: a stripe pattern at 1.3 source pixels per period is below the source grid's Nyquist limit. The 160px grid cannot represent it. Which phase the stripes happen to land on, frame by frame, decides whether they alias to a strong pattern or cancel to mush — and the upscaler faithfully upscales whichever aliased result it was handed. Ground truth, sampled on the 320px grid, represents the same stripes correctly and only varies by where the edges sit.

That is why the effect is not monotone. Sweeping stripe period from 2.0 to 12.0 px and recording the up/GT ratio gives, in order, 0.47, 1.75, 0.78, 2.25, 1.60, 1.18, 1.17, 1.26, 1.51 — seven of nine above 1, median 1.26, but two well below. It is a beat pattern, not a threshold. Any claim of the form "per-frame upscaling is more/less temporally stable" is a statement about where the content sits relative to the source grid's Nyquist limit, and without that qualifier the number is not portable between scenes. My earlier table was measured on a scene without the aliasing term; this one includes it; that is the whole difference.

Two things survive intact. First, the CV-of-a-frame-statistic family of metrics is not trustworthy on moving content — that is what the speed axis and the kernel axis both show, and the aliasing term makes it worse by adding a second uncontrolled variable. Second, the practical advice does not depend on resolving this: detail at or below two source pixels per cycle cannot be recovered at any scale factor, and it is exactly that detail which makes temporal stability numbers jump around. If your source has it, fix the source or accept the crawl. No kernel setting reaches it.
